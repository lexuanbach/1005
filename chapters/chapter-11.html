Chapter 11 · Computing Track · Week 47

Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence sounds like magic until you see it as a function: something goes in (x), something comes out (Y), and the part in between (F) was not written line by line but learned from data. This chapter says what AI is and is not, where it is used (including at HCMUT), and then opens the box with a tiny example: a straight line that learns house prices. You will see the difference between training and inference with your own eyes.

01 · Learn the concepts

From rules to learning: what AI is, and how it learns

11.1What is AI — and what it is not

Artificial intelligence is the field of building computer systems that perform tasks we normally take as signs of intelligence: seeing and understanding the world, recognising speech, understanding language, learning from experience, reasoning, making decisions, and acting. The usual starting point is the Dartmouth workshop of 1956, whose proposal was written by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon. The slides put it as “giving machines human capabilities”.

Two warnings from the textbook. First, AI is not a copy of the human brain: it is a collection of computing methods, and a system that is superb at reading licence plates may understand nothing else. Second, there is no single agreed definition; think of AI as a general-purpose technology, like electricity.

Why does AI exist at all? Because you already know what classical programs are good at: following clear instructions. if (temperature < 18) heaterOn(); works perfectly. But try writing the rules that tell a dog from a cat in any photo, or that capture what a vague sentence means. Every rule you write has exceptions (a tiny dog, a cat with no visible whiskers, a blurry shot). That gap, tasks that are easy for people and impossible to spell out as rules, is the AI problem. It is also why “doing arithmetic very fast” does not count as intelligence: a calculator just runs one procedure someone wrote.

… every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described …

From the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth summer project by McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon, quoted from Wikipedia — Dartmouth workshop. The sentence goes on to say that a machine could then be made to simulate it: the founding conjecture of AI.
Did you know? The proposal asked for “a 2-month, 10-man study” in the summer of 1956, a modest plan for a field that now employs millions. Early programs from that era, such as the Logic Theorist (it proved mathematical theorems) and ELIZA (it imitated a therapist's chat), were impressive but worked only in very narrow, tidy situations. The founders did not fail because the idea was wrong; they underestimated how complicated intelligence is. Source: Wikipedia — Dartmouth workshop.

11.2Six abilities of intelligence — and the size of the impact

If we ask what special abilities humans actually have, the textbook (and the slides) list six. Today's AI is uneven across them:

AbilityWhat it meansWhere AI stands
ReasoningRepresenting knowledge and deducing new facts from it: “all students submit before the deadline; An is a student, so An submits before the deadline”.The weakest; long, consistent, checkable reasoning is the main research frontier.
LearningAcquiring skills from experience, a lifelong process for people.The key switch from ordinary programs to modern AI (see 11.6).
VisionSeeing and understanding the physical world, not only capturing light.Very strong, but can fail on noisy, rare or unfamiliar images.
ActionRobots, movement, physical coordination, driving.Progressing, but the real world adds friction, delay, noisy sensors and surprises.
LanguageUnderstanding documents, summarising, writing; hard because of ambiguity and context.Very strong at fluent text; fluent is not always correct (hallucination).
CommunicationSpeech recognition, speaking, keeping a conversation going.Strong; understanding intent and tone is still harder than transcription.

The slides also draw the chain data → information → knowledge → intelligence; we meet it again in 11.5. The slides say reasoning from knowledge is the weakest ability, the one the research community is chasing hardest. (The slide also gives language and vision “8–9 out of 10”: that is an informal impression, not a measured score, so we do not repeat it as a number.)

Impact. AI could raise productivity, changes a large share of work tasks, and raises questions about inequality and new skills. Everyone is not equally ready: many people still lack internet access or digital skills, so the benefits are not shared automatically. The textbook cites an IMF analysis showing that richer economies have more jobs exposed to AI, but exposed does not mean replaced.

Slide correction · a number without a source Slide 16 shows “+$2.6–4.4 trillion · projected annual boost to the global economy”, “expected increase in global GDP over the next decade”, and a claim about 80% of the global workforce, with no source. We traced them: the $2.6–4.4 trillion figure is from a 2023 McKinsey Global Institute report, which estimated that generative AI could add the equivalent of that much value per year across the 63 use cases it analysed; it is a potential with many assumptions, not a GDP forecast. A related OpenAI study said that about 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their tasks affected by large language models: a statement about exposure, in one country. The slide's “global GDP over the next decade” and “80% of the global workforce” are not what those sources say, and we could not find a source for them, so please do not quote them. McKinsey · “GPTs are GPTs” (Eloundou et al.).

11.4Generative AI and four milestones

A spam filter answers “is this email spam?”. A generative model writes a new email, draws a picture or produces code from a prompt (the request you type). The distinction is the kind of output (a decision about existing data versus new content), not a hard line between model designs. Four milestones from the slides, with dates we checked:

WhenMilestoneWhy it mattered
2012AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton), a convolutional neural network, wins the ImageNet image-recognition challengeTop-5 error 15.3%, more than 10.8 percentage points better than the runner-up: deep learning took over computer vision.
2017Transformer (“Attention Is All You Need”, Vaswani et al., on arXiv in June 2017)Attention lets a model weigh every other word in the context; one architecture then served text, images and more.
30 Nov 2022ChatGPT released to the publicMillions of ordinary people could talk to an AI in plain language; generative AI went mainstream.
20 Jan 2025DeepSeek-R1 releasedStrong reasoning models at a lower cost than many earlier large models: AI is getting cheaper and easier to deploy.

Underneath, a large language model works by predicting the most likely next piece of text given the context, which is why it can sound right without being right: that is hallucination. (For the textbook: GPT is mainly used to generate text; BERT mainly to understand it, e.g. classify or extract.) Chapter 12 goes deeper into LLMs and their security.

Slide corrections (1) Slide 12 credits the Transformer with “self-supervised learning that removed the labelling bottleneck”. The 2017 paper itself trained on labelled translation pairs; self-supervised pre-training (hide a word, predict it) is how later models such as BERT and GPT exploit Transformers. (2) Slide 14's “definitive rise of high-performance, low-cost AI” is a trend, and the textbook states it more carefully: R1 shows strong reasoning models at lower cost than many earlier large ones. (3) Slide 11 mentions “1D, 2D and 3D convolutions”: 1D and 3D convolutions exist (signals, video), but AlexNet's convolutions are 2D, over images.
Did you know? ChatGPT is reported to have gained one million users in five days and about 100 million in two months, one of the fastest-growing consumer applications ever seen. Before that, AI already filtered your spam, recommended videos and unlocked phones; people just did not feel they were “talking to an AI”. Source: Wikipedia — ChatGPT.

11.5AI as a function: Y = F(x), training and inference

The most useful sentence in the chapter: AI is a function. x is the input (an image, a sentence, a signal), F is the model, and Y is the output (a prediction, a recommendation, new content, an action). If you have written int square(int n) in Chapter 6, you already know what a function is; the new thing is that here nobody writes the body of F by hand. Its parameters (numbers inside the model) are learned from examples. That gives the two phases the syllabus asks for:

TrainingInference
What happensShow examples, measure the error, adjust the parameters, repeatGive the finished model a new x and read off Y
ParametersChangeFrozen
NeedsLots of data, an error measure, lots of computing (GPUs for big models)The model and the input, one pass
Cost and frequencyExpensive, done now and then (and again when the model needs updating)Cheap, done every time someone uses it
ExampleFitting a spam filter on 100,000 labelled emailsClassifying the email that just arrived

From data to intelligence. The textbook separates four words people mix up. Data is raw observation (pixels, sensor readings, characters). Information is data organised to answer a question (today's average temperature). Knowledge links information with context and rules (a sudden rise can mean a fault). Intelligence uses knowledge to reason, decide and act. Modern systems are very good at the first steps, spotting patterns in data, and still struggle with reliable knowledge and reasoning. Y = F(x) is also not magic: it is a computed mapping, and the hard part is making F generalise to new inputs and not produce dangerous or biased answers.

Learning for life? Not always The slides compare machine learning with the Vietnamese saying “Học, học nữa, học mãi” and ABET's “lifelong learning”. Nice analogy, with a correction from the textbook: many deployed models do not keep learning. They are trained, then used as they are; to improve them, people update the data and retrain or fine-tune. And machine “learning” is optimisation of parameters on data, not the embodied, social learning people do.

11.6From rules to learning — a rule-based program versus a learned one

Machine learning (ML) means the computer learns from data instead of only following rules a person wrote. Here is the contrast in the language you know. The rule-based spam filter is pure Chapter 3: a human chooses every threshold. The learned filter has the same shape (a function from two numbers to a yes/no) but the numbers inside it come from training on labelled emails, such as the ones in Panel 2 of the lab:

rule_based_filter.cpp — a human picks every number
bool spamByRules(int links, int capsWords) {
    if (links > 5)                    return true;
    if (capsWords > 6)                return true;
    if (links > 3 && capsWords > 3) return true;
    return false;
}   // spammers adapt: now someone must rewrite the rules
learned_filter.cpp — the numbers come from training
bool spamByModel(double links, double caps) {
    double w1 = 10.14, w2 = 11.07, b = -11.00;   // learned
    return w1 * links / 10 + w2 * caps / 10 + b >= 0;
}   // retrain on new data: new w1, w2, b — same code

Try the email with 5 links and 4 capital words: the hand-written rules say spam (the third rule fires); the learned weights (these are what logistic regression finds after 600 training steps on the lab's data) say not spam, because they learned that both numbers must be high. Neither is “the truth”; each is only as good as the data or the rules behind it. The learned program must also generalise: do well on new emails, not just remember the training ones.

Three (plus two) ways to learn:

KindDataGoalExample
SupervisedLabelled: each example has the right answerPredict the label for new inputsSpam or not; house price; dog or cat
UnsupervisedNo labelsFind structure in the dataGrouping shoppers into regulars, bargain hunters, big spenders
ReinforcementRewards and penalties from actingLearn a strategy by trial and errorA game-playing AI; a robot learning to walk
Semi-supervisedA little labelled, a lot unlabelledCut the cost of labellingMedical images
Self-supervisedLabels made from the data itselfLearn from huge unlabelled dataHide a word, predict it: how BERT and GPT are pre-trained

Features and models. A model never sees the world, only a representation of it. In traditional ML people design the features (links, ALL-CAPS words, email length, sender domain). In deep learning the model learns its own features from raw data: edges first, then parts, then whole objects. Deep models are powerful for images, audio and language, but harder to explain, because their internal features do not always match concepts people understand.

11.7Training versus inference with a line — house prices

The textbook's example: Mr X wants to buy a 140 m² house and has five recent sales (size in m², price in thousand $). The more data points are bigger houses with higher prices, so he guesses a straight line y = a·x + b: here F is the line and a, b are its two parameters. “Learning” means choosing them well.

x (m²)actual yline y = 3x + 3error = actual − predicted
902802737
100295303−8
1103403337

The initial guess y = 3x + 3 came from the slope between the first and last house, (400 − 280)/(130 − 90) = 3, and the intercept that makes the line pass through the average point (110, 333). It misses every house a little (Table 4.2 of the textbook; the 120 m² house sold for 350 but the line says 363).

To score a line, square each error (so + and − cannot cancel, and big misses count more) and add: L = 7² + (−8)² + 7² + (−13)² + 7² = 380. This loss is the number training wants to make small.

Training by gradient descent. Ask: if I nudge a up a little, does L go up or down? That slope is the gradient. At (a, b) = (3, 3) the textbook finds dL/da = 100 (positive: increasing a makes things worse) and dL/db = 0. So move a against the gradient with a small step (the learning rate η = 0.00001): a ← 3 − 0.00001·100 = 2.999, b unchanged. Repeat. Here is exactly that loop in C++ (your Chapter 4 loops, plus a little calculus):

learn_a_line.cpp — training, then inference
double x[5] = {90, 100, 110, 120, 130};      // size in m2
double y[5] = {280, 295, 340, 350, 400};     // price in thousand $
double a = 3, b = 3;                         // the parameters: start at y = 3x + 3
double eta = 0.00001;                        // learning rate

for (int step = 1; step <= 5; step++) {      // TRAINING
    double da = 0, db = 0, loss = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < 5; i++) {
        double err = y[i] - (a * x[i] + b);  // actual - predicted
        loss += err * err;
        da += -2 * x[i] * err;           // dL/da
        db += -2 * err;                  // dL/db
    }
    cout << "step " << step << ": a=" << a << " b=" << b << " L=" << loss << endl;
    a -= eta * da;                       // move against the gradient
    b -= eta * db;
}
cout << a * 140 + b << endl;             // INFERENCE: parameters frozen, new x = 140

Run (with fixed and setprecision(5)) it prints L = 380, 379.9615, 379.95945, … and the final line gives about 422.9 for the 140 m² house: after five tiny steps the line has barely moved, exactly as in the textbook's Table 4.3. Real training runs thousands or millions of steps. The exact best line for these five houses, from a formula, is a = 2.95, b = 8.5 with L = 377.5, which predicts 421.5 thousand $ (about $421,500). That last step, plugging in 140 without changing anything, is inference.

Two more lessons from the textbook's exercise. (1) Add data, retrain: two more sales (95 m² for 300 and 125 m² for 360) change the best line to roughly y = 2.66x + 40.1 and the 140 m² prediction to about 411.8. A model is only as current as its data. (2) Choose the right shape: for the invented ultra-luxury villas (200 m² for $11.6 M up to 1,800 m² for $320.6 M) a straight line has big systematic errors, while a quadratic y = ax² + bx + c (three parameters, same training idea) follows the curve. Exercise 6 checks the textbook's villa example, and finds an error in it. Panel 1 of the lab below has both datasets.

Did you know? Supplementary “Fit a line by minimising the squared errors” is older than computers by two centuries: Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805 (to fit comet orbits), and Gauss claimed to have used it since 1795 and published his own account in 1809, which started a priority dispute. For a line there is a closed formula, as the lab's “best line” button uses. Gradient descent earns its keep on models with no formula. Source: Wikipedia — Least squares (history).

11.8Data is the fuel — and biased data bends the model

Data lets algorithms find patterns, and the explosion of data (messages, photos, sensors, phones, cars, factories) is a big reason modern AI took off. But good data is not just a lot of data: it must be correct, representative, diverse and relevant to the goal. A million wrongly labelled photos can teach a worse model than ten thousand carefully labelled ones; a leaf-disease model trained on a few dozen lab photos may fail in a muddy field. The textbook describes big data by four Vs:

VMeaningAI angle
VolumeHow much dataMore examples help, if they are good.
VelocityHow fast it arrives and must be processedStreaming sensors, live traffic.
VarietyText, images, video, audio, signalsModels must cope with many forms.
VeracityHow trustworthy and representative it isLarge but wrong or skewed data gives a wrong or skewed model.

Biased data, biased model. A model learns the patterns it is shown, including social bias. And removing the obvious column does not fix it: other features can act as proxies for it (school names, activities, wording, career gaps may correlate with gender, region or income). The same worry applies to education: a model that predicts student ability from past data can disadvantage students from under-resourced schools, students who work part-time, or those with poor internet. Panel 2 of the lab lets you watch this: switch on a skewed sample and watch the boundary bend so that legitimate newsletters get blocked.

True story · Amazon's recruiting experiment Reuters reported in 2018 that Amazon had built an experimental tool to rate job candidates from one to five stars. It was trained on patterns in resumes submitted over ten years, most of them from men, and Amazon's engineers found it did not rate candidates for technical roles in a gender-neutral way. The team was disbanded; Amazon said recruiters never relied on it alone. The lesson is not that “AI is evil”: the data was a faithful picture of an unequal past. Source: Reuters (via CNBC), 10 October 2018.
Did you know? Supplementary Learning machines are older than deep learning. The Mark I Perceptron, built by Frank Rosenblatt's team at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in 1959 and first shown to the public in June 1960, looked at a 20×20 grid of 400 photocells to recognise images. It is the ancestor of the single “neuron” that the classifier in lab Panel 2 resembles. Source: Wikipedia — Perceptron.

11.9AI, ML, DL — and using AI responsibly

These three words are not synonyms; they are nested. AI is the broad goal of machines showing intelligent behaviour, built by rules, search, logic, probability, learning, or a mix. Machine learning is the way of building AI from data. Deep learning is machine learning with many-layered neural networks. So not every AI system learns (a chess program that searches moves, or an expert system with hand-written rules, is AI but not ML), and not every ML model is deep. A plain thermostat that follows “if below the threshold, heat” is a reflex agent but usually too simple to call AI today.

An artificial “neuron” is a big simplification of a biological one: it multiplies inputs by weights, adds them up, passes the sum through an activation function and hands the result on. Deep learning is not a copy of the brain; it needs lots of data and computing, and is hard to explain. AI is also classified by ability: narrow AI (good at specific tasks, all that exists today), AGI (any human-level intellectual task: not achieved), and ASI (hypothetical, beyond humans); and by function: expert systems, predictive AI, generative AI.

Using AI in your own studies. The textbook's rule: AI can be an assistant, never the person responsible. Good uses: ask it to explain a concept at several levels, then check the answer against the book, rewrite it in your own words, and ask “what is the weakness in that answer?” or “give a counter-example”; generate review questions; compare concepts; find holes in your argument. Poor uses: letting it read for you, do the assignment for you, or giving you answers you do not understand. A fluent answer that is wrong in substance is more dangerous than an awkward one you verified.

RiskWhat it looks likeA sensible habit
PrivacyFace recognition in public places without consent; voice assistants recording more than you thinkAsk who can access the data, where it is stored, and for what purpose.

05 · Team activity

AI project canvas: a campus problem, on one page

Teams of 3–4 · one class period to prepare · a 5-minute presentation. Counts toward the class-activities 10%.

The task

Pick one real problem on campus that an AI function Y = F(x) could help with (examples: predicting which library study rooms will be full, sorting student-service emails, spotting broken lab equipment from photos, helping new students find information, estimating bus waiting time). Fill in the AI project canvas below on one page and present it in five minutes. You are not building anything: you are showing that you can think about an AI system the way an engineer does.

Canvas boxYour team writes
1. The problem and the userWho is helped, and what decision or task gets easier?
2. Input x and output YExactly what goes in (text, image, numbers?) and what comes out (a label, a number, text, an action?)
3. Rules or learning?Could a rule-based C++ program do it? If not, why not? Which kind of learning (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement)?
4. DataWhere does the training data come from, who labels it, and how many examples? Is it representative of all users? Any privacy concern?
5. Training versus inferenceWhat would you train on, how often would you retrain, and what happens at inference time (on a server or on the device)?
6. What could go wrongBias and proxy features, hallucination or wrong answers, privacy, misuse. Name at least one for each of two groups of users.
7. Success and human in the loopHow will you measure success (and for which groups)? When must a human check or decide, and how can a user appeal?

Constraints

  • One page and five minutes: choose; do not list everything.
  • Boxes 2 and 4 must be concrete. “Student data” is not an answer; “each row = one booking: room, hour, weekday, exam week yes/no” is.
  • You must propose one measurable success target (for example “at least 90% of predicted-full rooms really are full”) and say how you would check it on data the model never trained on (generalisation).
  • You must include one place where a person overrules the AI.
