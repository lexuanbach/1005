Draw & Teach a Brain

Paint coloured dots, build a tiny neural network, press Train and watch it learn where one colour ends and the next begins. Then switch to Predict, freeze the brain, and ask it about dots it has never seen. This is the chapter's idea Y = F(x) with F learned from data, in two dimensions you can see. (Chapter 11, sections 11.5 to 11.9.)

A toy, not ChatGPT. This brain has between 6 and about 200 numbers (weights) and learns to colour a flat picture. A chat model like ChatGPT is the same kind of idea (numbers adjusted to reduce an error) scaled up by many orders of magnitude and trained on text. Nothing here talks, reasons or understands.

What am I looking at?
The coloured dots
The data. Each dot is one example: two numbers in (x, how far right, and y, how high) and one label out (its colour). This is the "data is the fuel" idea from 11.8. You are the data scientist: what you paint is all the brain will ever know.
The coloured background
The function F that the network has learned, drawn for every point of the board. Each pixel asks the brain "which colour would you give a dot here?". A muddy blend means "not sure".
Filled dots versus hollow dots
Training dots are the only ones the weights are adjusted on. Test dots are held back and only used to check. If the brain does well on training dots but badly on hollow ones, it has memorised instead of learned (overfitting).
The network diagram
Inputs x, y go in on the left; each neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up, and passes the sum through an activation function (11.9). The lines are the weights, which are what training changes. Each layer has its own colour (see the legend under the diagram); a thicker line is a bigger weight and a dashed line is a negative one. With no hidden layer the brain can only draw straight lines; hidden layers let it bend them.
Inspect a dot
Training in slow motion for one dot. Forward: the dot’s two numbers flow right; every neuron adds up (input × weight) + bias and squashes the sum. Loss: the output is compared with the dot’s true colour (cross-entropy). Backward: the error flows left; each weight gets a gradient (its share of the blame; thicker line = bigger, dashed = negative, shown in the gradient colour). Update: each weight moves a little against its gradient, by the learning rate. Real training averages 32 dots per step before moving.
The loss curve
The error that training is trying to push down (cross-entropy: a number that is large when the brain is confidently wrong). Each step moves the weights downhill, exactly the loop of 11.7 but with dozens of weights instead of two. If the learning rate is too large, the steps overshoot and the loss climbs instead.
Predict (frozen)
Inference: the weights are locked, no learning happens, and the brain just applies the function it already has to new inputs. Same F, new x.
Biased data
The brain never sees which village an applicant is from, but the position on the board acts as a proxy. With a skewed sample the average accuracy looks great while one group is treated badly. This is the 11.8 lesson: overall accuracy hides who is being failed, so check results by group.

Honesty note: everything runs in your browser on invented data, with a network of at most 24 hidden neurons. It is a simulation of the training idea, not a model of the human brain and not a language model.
