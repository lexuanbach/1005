Network Builder

Wire up small networks like a network engineer. Place switches, routers, firewalls and access points, give every device an IP address, mask and gateway, then press Show or Send: a packet walks the network hop by hop, and the simulator says exactly why a ping worked or failed. Eight levels, from two PCs and a switch to a dorm floor on a budget, plus a sandbox.

How to play: drag devices, join them with the Cable tool (or the Connect button in the inspector), click a device to edit its settings. Green goals mean you solved that requirement; finish them all to complete the level. It is a simulation: it is the rules of Chapter 9 (subnets, gateways, switches vs routers, NAT, DNS, firewalls), not a real network. The concepts are explained in Chapter 9.

What am I looking at?
Hosts (PC, laptop, server)
End devices (§9.3). Each has an IP address and a subnet mask; the mask decides which addresses are "on my own LAN". Anything else goes to the default gateway.
Switch and hub
Layer-2 boxes (§9.3). A switch forwards a frame only to the right port; a hub copies it to everyone (one collision domain). Neither joins different subnets, and three switches in a ring cause a broadcast storm.
Router
Layer-3 box. Every port belongs to one subnet and has an address in it. It forwards by destination IP: a connected subnet first, otherwise the default route. Turning on NAT rewrites private source addresses so the ISP does not drop them (§9.5).
Firewall
Filters packets by protocol and port, first matching rule wins, otherwise the default policy (§9.3). Port 80 is the web, port 23 is Telnet (plain-text passwords).
Wi-Fi access point
Joins wireless laptops to the wired LAN (§9.4). Dashed lines are Wi-Fi.
ISP / Internet
The cloud on the right is an invented Internet that uses documentation addresses (192.0.2.0/24, 198.51.100.0/24, 203.0.113.0/24, RFC 5737), like the Packet Journey lab. Names such as www.example.com need a DNS server (198.51.100.53).
Colours
Every device type has its own colour (see the key under the board; a small number on a device tells apart several of the same type). The packet is a dot in the colour of the device that is sending it on this hop, and the cable it crosses lights up in that colour; its label says what it carries (DNS query, request, reply). Cables are grey until a packet uses them. The green tick or red cross shows where the run ended; they always mean success or failure.
Animation controls
Under the board: Pause / Resume and Step (one hop at a time while paused), Skip, and a six-level Speed slider (Instant shows only the result). While the packet is at a device, a pop-over shows what happens inside it: the switch learns and forwards, the hub repeats to everyone, the router looks up the route and lowers the TTL, the firewall matches a rule, NAT rewrites the source. The header strip shows the packet's protocol, source, destination and TTL, coloured by layer (IP address and TTL: layer 3; protocol and port: layer 4, as its small key says); a field flashes when it changes (TTL at every router, the source address and port at NAT, the destination coming back).
What is left out
ARP and MAC tables (a "who has this address?" broadcast simply finds the owner), random packet loss, routing protocols, IPv6, TCP handshakes. Firewall and NAT are stateful: replies to an allowed request always come back.

Related: the Packet Journey lab in Chapter 9 follows a single web request through DNS, TCP and HTTP; Network Builder is about the network the packet has to cross.
